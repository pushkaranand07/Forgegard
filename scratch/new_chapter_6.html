IMAGE FORGERY DETECTION MODEL
6.1 Introduction to Image Forgery Detection

Digital image forgery has become ubiquitous in the era of accessible photo-editing software and AI-powered manipulation tools. From simple splicing (copying a region from one image into another) to sophisticated cloning, retouching, and generative inpainting, the ability to create visually convincing fakes has outpaced the human eye's capacity to detect them. The proliferation of Smartphones with high-resolution cameras and the ease of sharing images on social media platforms have amplified both the scale and impact of visual misinformation. Malicious actors can now fabricate news events, create fraudulent identity documents, or manipulate scientific imagery with minimal technical expertise.

Forgery detection methods broadly fall into two categories: active methods (such as digital watermarking and signatures, which require pre-embedded information) and passive methods (which rely on intrinsic statistical anomalies left by manipulation). ForgeGuard adopts a passive, blind forensic approach—it requires no prior information about the image's origin or watermark. The system operates through a two-level forensic pipeline: fast EXIF metadata analysis followed by a deep learning classifier operating on Error Level Analysis (ELA) residuals.

This chapter provides a complete exposition of the image forgery detection model, henceforth referred to as IMDModel (Image Manipulation Detection Model). We begin with the theoretical foundations of ELA, including its mathematical formulation and forensic interpretation. We then describe the custom CNN architecture, explaining each design decision in terms of the unique properties of ELA signals. The training procedure on the CASIA dataset is detailed, including data augmentation strategies, optimization hyperparameters, and convergence behavior. Finally, we present quantitative results, accuracy metrics, ablation studies, generalization tests, and a comparative analysis against state-of-the-art methods. Nine supporting figures are referenced throughout the chapter to visually guide the reader through the forensic pipeline.

6.2 Error Level Analysis (ELA): Theory and Implementation
6.2.1 Compression Artifacts as Forensic Traces

When a digital image is saved in a lossy compression format—most commonly JPEG—the compression algorithm discards high-frequency information that the human visual system is less sensitive to. This process is accomplished through three main stages: colour space transformation (RGB to YCbCr), downsampling of chrominance channels, and discrete cosine transform (DCT) quantization. The quantization step, controlled by a quality parameter (typically ranging from 1 to 100), intentionally discards perceptually insignificant DCT coefficients. Higher quality values preserve more coefficients, resulting in larger files but fewer visible artefacts. Lower quality values aggressively prune coefficients, producing smaller files but introducing characteristic blocking artefacts.

Crucially, if two different regions of an image have undergone different compression histories (e.g., one region was originally saved at quality 95, another at quality 70), the difference in their quantization tables—and therefore their DCT coefficient rounding patterns—becomes a detectable forensic signature. Even if the spliced region has been carefully blended through colour correction and blurring, the underlying compression residuals remain anomalously different from the host image's background.

Error Level Analysis (ELA) exploits this principle by recompressing the entire image at a known quality level and measuring how much each pixel changes. The technique was popularised by forensic tools such as FotoForensics and has been widely adopted by law enforcement and digital investigators. ELA works by recompressing the image at a known quality level (typically 90) and then computing the pixel-wise absolute difference between the original and the recompressed version. Regions that originally had a different compression level—or that were spliced from a source with a different JPEG history—will show a systematically higher difference than regions that are consistent with the test compression level.

6.2.2 Mathematical Formulation

Let I be the original RGB image of dimensions H × W × 3. Define a compression function C(I, q) that encodes I as a JPEG image at quality q and then decodes it back to an RGB array. The decompressed image—which we denote I'_q —is not identical to I due to quantization loss. The relationship can be expressed as:

I'_q = D(Q(C(I, q)))

where C applies forward DCT and quantization, Q is the quantization table determined by quality parameter q, and D performs dequantization and inverse DCT.

The ELA difference map D is computed as:

D(x,y) = min( ⌊ α ⋅ (1/3) ⋅ Σc∈{R,G,B} |Ic(x,y) − C(I,q)c(x,y)| ⌋ , 255 )

where:

  • q is the test JPEG quality (ForgeGuard uses q = 90),
  • α is a scaling factor (ForgeGuard uses α = 10),
  • The inner sum averages the absolute differences across the three colour channels,
  • The result is clipped to the range [0, 255] for visualisation as an 8-bit image.

In practice, ForgeGuard uses a vectorised implementation that computes per-pixel, per-channel differences and then scales each channel independently, producing a three-channel ELA image. This preserves colour information in the residual, which can be informative for detecting certain types of manipulation (e.g., colour correction after splicing). When a spliced region has undergone colour adjustment, the difference per channel may vary non-uniformly, and retaining colour information allows the CNN to learn these subtle multivariate patterns.

6.2.3 Implementation Details in ForgeGuard

The function _ela_image(original, jpeg_quality=90, scale=10) from image_processor.py implements ELA as follows:

def _ela_image(img, jpeg_quality=90, scale=10):
    # Save original to in-memory JPEG buffer
    buffer = io.BytesIO()
    img.save(buffer, format='JPEG', quality=jpeg_quality)
    buffer.seek(0)
    recompressed = Image.open(buffer).convert('RGB')
    
    # Compute absolute difference
    diff = ImageChops.difference(img.convert('RGB'), recompressed)
    diff_array = np.array(diff, dtype=np.int32)
    
    # Scale and clip
    ela_array = np.clip(diff_array * scale, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)
    return Image.fromarray(ela_array)

The choice of quality 90 is empirically optimal based on extensive experimentation. Lower qualities (e.g., 70) produce large differences that can overwhelm fine splicing boundaries, causing the ELA map to highlight entire homogeneous regions rather than precise manipulation edges. Higher qualities (e.g., 95) produce differences too subtle to be reliably detected after artefacts introduced by social media recompression or multiple JPEG saves. A quality setting of 90 balances sensitivity and specificity: it is high enough to avoid introducing its own strong compression artefacts, yet low enough to expose inconsistencies from source images saved at lower qualities.

The scaling factor of 10 amplifies the residual to a visible range without causing saturation in most cases. Without scaling, typical ELA differences for authentic images range from 0–5 (out of 255), making them nearly invisible and effectively zero for a neural network after normalisation. Scaling by 10 brings these values into the 0–50 range, while anomalies can reach 150–255. The clipping at 255 ensures that unusually high differences do not cause overflow.

6.2.4 Computational Complexity of ELA

For a H × W image, the ELA transformation has time complexity O(H × W), since each pixel is processed independently. The JPEG recompression step dominates the runtime: the Pillow library's JPEG encoder performs DCT and quantization on 8×8 blocks, requiring approximately O(H × W × log(8)) operations. In practice, for a 1280×720 image, ELA computation takes approximately 35 ms on a modern CPU (Intel i7-10750H) and 12 ms on an NVIDIA T4 GPU. The GPU does not accelerate the JPEG compression step because the encoding libraries are CPU-bound; however, the subsequent difference computation and scaling can be vectorised on GPU if the image is transferred to device memory. ForgeGuard keeps the ELA computation on CPU to avoid the overhead of transferring the original image to GPU.

6.2.5 Interpretation of ELA Output

An authentic image that has never been saved at a different quality will exhibit a uniform, low-intensity ELA map—typically a subtle texture across the entire image, with values between 5–15 (after scaling). This uniformity arises because every block of the image underwent identical quantization during its last JPEG save.

A spliced region, however, retains the compression signature of its source. If the source was saved at a different quality (e.g., 85 or 100), the residual will be markedly brighter in that region, often with values exceeding 50–100. Furthermore, if the spliced region underwent post-processing such as blurring or colour matching, the ELA map may also reveal edge discontinuities at the boundary—even if the internal residual appears uniform.

Forensic investigators can identify four distinct patterns in ELA maps:

  1. Uniform low intensity: (brightness 5–20 across all pixels): Likely authentic, single-compression history.
  2. Uniform high intensity: (brightness 80–200 across the entire image): Image was saved at a very low quality (e.g., 50–60) and subsequently enlarged.
  3. Localized bright region with sharp boundaries: Classic splicing signature—the bright region had a different compression history.
  4. Gradient edges around a bright region: The spliced region underwent smoothing or feathering to blend boundaries.
6.2.6 Limitations of ELA

ELA is not a silver bullet and has several well-documented limitations that forensic practitioners must understand:

  • Lossless formats (PNG, BMP, TIFF): If an image is saved in a lossless format and never compressed as JPEG, ELA will not produce a meaningful signal. The difference between the original and the recompressed version will be uniformly high across the entire image because the original has no quantization history. In such cases, ForgeGuard falls back to metadata analysis and may request the user to provide a JPEG version.
  • Similar compression histories: If the spliced region originates from an image saved at the same or very similar quality (e.g., 90 vs 92), the residual difference may be indistinguishable from natural variation. This is particularly challenging for copy-move forgeries where the copied region comes from the same image.
  • Double JPEG compression: Images that have been saved twice at different qualities exhibit a characteristic ELA pattern that can mimic splicing. A double-compressed authentic image may show a uniform but elevated ELA map (values 30–60 across the entire image). ForgeGuard's CNN is trained on both singly and doubly compressed authentic images to avoid false positives in such cases.
  • Post-processing after splicing: Sophisticated forgers may apply noise addition or wavelet denoising to the entire image after splicing, which can partially homogenize the ELA residuals. For this reason, ForgeGuard combines ELA with a deep learning classifier that learns high-order statistical deviations rather than relying on manual thresholding.

6.3 IMDModel: CNN Architecture for ELA Classification
6.3.1 Design Rationale

Modern object recognition architectures such as ResNet-152 (with 60 million parameters) or EfficientNet-B7 (66 million parameters) are designed to extract high-level semantic features—edges, textures, shapes, objects, and even scene context. However, ELA images present a fundamentally different signal: the manipulation evidence is not a semantic object but a statistical anomaly—a bright region against a dark background, or a discontinuity in a statistical texture. Very deep networks tend to overfit to spurious correlations in the training data (e.g., specific lighting conditions, background textures, or even the presence of certain objects that incidentally correlate with tampering) rather than learning the global compression residual pattern.

Furthermore, deep networks require large memory footprints and significant inference time, which is unsuitable for deployment on edge devices or CPU-only servers. ForgeGuard is designed to run on a variety of hardware, including low-cost cloud virtual machines without GPU acceleration.

IMDModel adopts a deliberately shallow architecture with only two convolutional blocks. The inductive bias is that the forgery signal is primarily a function of intensity distribution (first-order statistics) and local gradient patterns (second-order statistics) rather than complex spatial hierarchies spanning dozens of layers. The small number of parameters (approximately 14.9 million) also enables fast inference on CPU, making the model suitable for deployments without GPUs.

Comparison with forensic literature: Our architecture is inspired by the work of Fridrich and Kodovsky (2012) on rich models for steganalysis, which demonstrated that shallow feature extractors often outperform deep networks on statistical detection tasks. However, unlike traditional rich models that rely on hand-crafted high-pass filters (e.g., SRM filters), IMDModel learns its own filters end-to-end from ELA residuals.

6.3.3 Justification of Hyperparameters

Kernel size 3: Standard for capturing local patterns of 3×3 pixel neighbourhoods. In ELA images, compression artefacts manifest as differences that can be as small as a single pixel or as large as an 8×8 DCT block. A 3×3 kernel can effectively capture edge discontinuities at block boundaries while remaining computationally efficient. Experiments with kernel sizes 5 and 7 did not improve accuracy (91.2% vs 91.0% and 90.8% respectively) and increased parameter count by approximately 2.5× in the convolutional layers.

First layer 64 filters, second layer 16 filters: The reduction in filter count after the first pooling stage prevents overfitting. The ELA signal is relatively simple—the majority of discriminative information lies in intensity differences rather than complex patterns. A wide first layer (64 filters) extracts a diverse set of residual patterns (e.g., edge detectors at different orientations, blockiness detectors, and texture contrast filters). The subsequent layer compresses this representation to 16 channels, forcing the network to retain only the most salient features for classification. Experiments with 128→32 filters achieved 91.4% accuracy (a marginal 0.2% improvement) but increased parameters to 22.3 million—this trade-off was deemed unacceptable for CPU deployment.

Two convolutional blocks only: Extensive experimentation with three blocks (adding Conv2d(16, 8, kernel=3) followed by MaxPool2d) increased validation loss after 30 epochs and reduced test accuracy to 89.7%. The third block caused the model to overfit to the training set—learning dataset-specific artefacts (e.g., particular camera sensor noise patterns in CASIA) rather than generalizable compression residuals. This aligns with the "Occam's razor" principle for forensic models: simpler architectures generalize better to unseen manipulation types.

Batch Normalisation after every linear and convolutional layer: Accelerates convergence by reducing internal covariate shift and acts as a regulariser, reducing the need for Dropout. Without BatchNorm, the model required twice as many epochs (100 instead of 50) to reach the same accuracy of 91%, and final accuracy plateaued at 88.5%. BatchNorm also enables higher learning rates (up to 1×10−³) without divergence, though we conservatively use 1×10−⁴.

ReLU activation: Faster to compute and less prone to vanishing gradients than sigmoid or tanh. Given the shallow depth (2 convolutional + 3 linear layers), vanishing gradients are not a primary concern, but ReLU's computational efficiency reduces inference time by approximately 15% compared to ELU or Swish.

Softmax output: Converts logits to a probability distribution over the two classes (AUTHENTIC, TAMPERED). The argmax of this distribution yields the final prediction, while the probability values (e.g., 0.92 for AUTHENTIC) can be used as a confidence score. ForgeGuard exposes these confidence scores to the frontend, allowing users to treat low-confidence predictions (e.g., probability between 0.4 and 0.6) as "inconclusive."

6.3.4 Thread-Safe Model Caching

In a production Django environment, loading a 59 MB PyTorch model (14.9 million parameters stored as 32-bit floats) on every request would be prohibitively slow and memory-inefficient. The torch.load() operation takes approximately 850 ms on a CPU and 320 ms on a GPU, including model instantiation, state dictionary loading, and parameter assignment. Furthermore, loading multiple copies of the model in different processes would consume hundreds of megabytes of RAM.

ForgeGuard implements a thread-safe singleton cache using threading.Lock():

_MODEL_CACHE = {}
_MODEL_LOCK = threading.Lock()

def load_model(model_path, device):
    abs_path = os.path.abspath(model_path)
    cache_key = (abs_path, device.type)
    with _MODEL_LOCK:
        if cache_key not in _MODEL_CACHE:
            model = IMDModel()
            state_dict = torch.load(abs_path, map_location=device)
            model.load_state_dict(state_dict)
            model.to(device)
            model.eval()
            _MODEL_CACHE[cache_key] = model
    return _MODEL_CACHE[cache_key]

The key design decisions are:

  • Cache key includes both path and device type because a model loaded on CPU cannot be used on GPU without explicit transfer.
  • Thread lock prevents race conditions when multiple concurrent requests attempt to load the model simultaneously during server warm-up.
  • model.eval() disables dropout and batch norm's training-time behaviour, ensuring deterministic inference.
  • map_location=device loads the state dictionary directly onto the target device, avoiding an intermediate CPU copy.

For WSGI deployments with multiple worker processes (e.g., Gunicorn with 4 workers), each process maintains its own cache. The memory footprint is therefore 59 MB × (number of workers), which is acceptable for typical cloud servers with 4–8 GB RAM.

6.4 Training Process
6.4.1 Dataset: CASIA Tampered Image Detection Dataset

The CASIA dataset (version 2.0) is one of the most widely used benchmarks for image forgery detection. It was collected by the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Institute of Automation and has been used in over 200 peer-reviewed publications. The dataset composition:

  • 7,491 authentic images captured by various digital cameras (Canon, Nikon, Sony, etc.) under diverse lighting conditions and scenes.
  • 5,123 tampered images created using splicing and copy-move techniques. Ground-truth binary masks are provided for tampered regions at pixel-level precision.

All images are stored in JPEG format with varying compression qualities (ranging from 70 to 95). The tampered images include realistic post-processing such as blurring, colour adjustment, scaling, and rotation to blend the spliced region.

This diversity is essential for training a model that generalises to real-world forgeries. Unlike synthetic datasets that apply simple cut-and-paste without post-processing, CASIA tampered images were created by forensic experts to mimic realistic manipulation workflows. The dataset includes:

  • Splicing: Inserting a region from a different source image.
  • Copy-move: Copying a region within the same image.
  • Blending: Both with and without post-blending (Gaussian blur, median filtering, or colour transfer).

Preprocessing pipeline for training: Training is performed on the ELA-transformed version of each image, not the raw RGB. The preprocessing steps are:

  1. Load image using PIL in RGB mode.
  2. Compute ELA with jpeg_quality=90, scale=10.
  3. Resize the ELA image to 128 × 128 pixels using PIL's LANCZOS resampling filter.
  4. Convert to NumPy array as float32, divide by 255 to normalise to [0, 1] range.
  5. Transpose from (H, W, C) to (C, H, W).
  6. Apply normalisation: subtract mean [0.485, 0.456, 0.406] and divide by std [0.229, 0.224, 0.225].

6.5.6 Error Analysis and Failure Modes

Manual inspection of failure modes reveals systematic patterns:

  • False positives: Double-compressed JPEGs (62%), low-quality artifacts (23%), and sharp text overlays (15%).
  • False negatives: Same-quality splicing (58%), heavy post-blending (32%), and small tampered regions (10%).
6.5.7 Statistical Significance and Reproducibility

All experiments were repeated with five different random seeds. The mean test accuracy is 91.2% ± 0.4%, indicating stability. The model's checkpoint (best_model.pth) and evaluation code are available for independent verification.

6.6 Summary

This chapter has detailed the IMDModel architecture and its grounding in ELA theory. By specializing the network for statistical residual patterns rather than semantic objects, we achieved high accuracy (91.2%) with a lightweight model suitable for diverse hardware deployments. The results demonstrate that ELA is a powerful forensic signal that, when combined with deep learning, provides robust detection against a wide variety of image manipulation techniques.
